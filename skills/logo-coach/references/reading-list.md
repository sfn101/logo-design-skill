# Reading List

Books to assign, with what each teaches and when to assign it. Everything here is paraphrased — when you
assign a book, describe the idea in your own words and point to the topic; never quote passages or reproduce
pages. If the designer wants the exact wording, send them to the book.

Assign **one** reading at a time, tied to a specific weakness seen in their work, with a small task attached
("read X, then redo Y"). Name a topic or section rather than a chapter number unless you're sure of it —
editions differ.

Titles and authors verified 2026-09-30; edition notes where checked.

## 1. Core list

### Alina Wheeler (with Rob Meyerson from the 6th ed.) — *Designing Brand Identity: A Comprehensive Guide to the World of Brands and Branding* (Wiley; 6th ed. 2024)
**Teaches:** the whole branding process as a professional practice — research, strategy, naming, identity
design, touchpoints, brand management — organised as short, self-contained spreads, plus many case studies.
**Assign when:** the designer's briefs are thin, they skip strategy, or they sell "brand kits" and need to see
what a real identity programme contains beyond the logo. Good for turning Etsy orders into proper briefs.
**Task idea:** pick one case study in their category, write the brief as the studio probably had it.

### David Airey — *Logo Design Love: A Guide to Creating Iconic Brand Identities* (Peachpit/New Riders; 2nd ed. 2014)
**Teaches:** a working designer's process — the brief, the client relationship, sketching, keeping it
simple and relevant, presenting, pricing — told through real projects by many designers.
**Assign when:** the designer is early in client work, struggles with briefs, pricing or presenting, or jumps
to the computer too soon. Also a friendly first book on the "classic" logo principles.
**Also by Airey:** *Identity Designed* (Rockport, 2019) — longer case studies of identity projects from studios
around the world; assign when they need to see complete processes, not just finished marks.

### Michael Evamy — *Logo: The Reference Guide to Symbols and Logotypes* (Laurence King; 1st ed. 2007, 2nd ed. 2021)
**Teaches:** a visual encyclopedia of marks sorted by form and theme (circles, crosses, letters, animals…) —
how many different ways designers have solved the same shape.
**Assign when:** the designer's range is narrow or their idea is "new" but probably isn't. Before sketching a
motif, have them look up that motif's section to see what exists (and what they must avoid resembling).

### Tom Geismar, Sagi Haviv & Ivan Chermayeff — *Identify: Basic Principles of Identity Design in the Iconic Trademarks of Chermayeff & Geismar* (HOW Books, 2011)
**Teaches:** how one of the most influential identity studios thinks — simplicity, distinctiveness, memorability,
appropriateness — shown through the stories behind their marks and the alternatives they rejected.
**Assign when:** the designer over-complicates, decorates instead of reducing, or can't defend why a mark is
right. The rejected-alternatives material is ideal for learning to edit.

### Paul Rand — *Thoughts on Design* (1947; reissued Chronicle Books 2014) and *A Designer's Art* (Yale University Press, 1985)
**Teaches:** a modernist's argument for form and content working together, play and humour in design, and — in
*A Designer's Art* — his thinking on what a logo can and cannot do and how meaning accrues to a mark over time.
**Assign when:** the designer expects the logo to explain everything, or needs a short, dense read on taste
and restraint. Rand's own presentation booklets are also worth finding online as a model for Stage 6.

### Aaron Draplin — *Draplin Design Co.: Pretty Much Everything* (Abrams, 2016)
**Teaches:** a working designer's life and methods — thick lines, vernacular inspiration, drawing from
Americana, running a small studio, the value of making lots of things for yourself.
**Assign when:** the designer needs energy and volume, is too precious, or is building a one-person business
(like selling identity packages). Good antidote to perfectionism.

### Michael Johnson — *Branding: In Five and a Half Steps* (Thames & Hudson, 2016)
**Teaches:** a clear branding process from research and strategy to design, rollout and evolution, with many
international examples; the "half step" is about living with and adjusting a brand after launch.
**Assign when:** the designer can draw but can't connect design choices to strategy, or needs a structure for
explaining their process to clients.

### Ellen Lupton — *Thinking with Type: A Critical Guide for Designers, Writers, Editors, and Students* (Princeton Architectural Press; 3rd ed. 2024)
**Teaches:** type fundamentals — letter anatomy, classification, families, spacing (kerning and tracking), text,
grids — with a critical view of why typographic choices matter.
**Assign when:** typography scores low: stock fonts, poor spacing, clashing pairings, or the designer can't
name what they're choosing. The sections on letter anatomy and spacing come first for logo work.

### Josef Albers — *Interaction of Color* (Yale University Press, 1963; 50th-anniversary edition 2013)
**Teaches:** colour is relative — the same colour changes with its neighbours — learned through hands-on
exercises rather than theory.
**Assign when:** colour choices are arbitrary, the palette falls apart on different backgrounds, or the
designer relies on colour to rescue a weak form. Pair with a one-colour drill (`practice-drills.md`).

## 2. For wordmarks and letterforms

- **Michael Evamy — *Logotype* (Laurence King, 2012).** A companion to *Logo* devoted to wordmarks, monograms and
  single-letter marks, grouped by approach. Assign first for any wordmark project: study how small typographic
  moves (a cut, a ligature, a swapped letter) create ownership.
- **Karen Cheng — *Designing Type* (Yale University Press; 2nd ed. 2020).** How letters are actually constructed —
  proportions, stroke contrast, how each letter relates to its family. Assign when custom letters look "drawn
  wrong" or letters in a wordmark don't feel like one family.
- **Doyald Young — *Logotypes & Letterforms* (1993).** A hand-letterer's close look at logotype craft and
  typographic detail. Assign for refinement once the idea is right.
- **Bruce Willen & Nolen Strals — *Lettering & Type: Creating Letters and Designing Typefaces* (Princeton
  Architectural Press, 2009).** A practical, approachable bridge between drawing letters and designing type.
  Assign when the designer wants to start hand-lettering their wordmarks.
- Lupton's *Thinking with Type* (above) for anatomy and spacing.

For "what should I read to get better at wordmarks?": Evamy's *Logotype* (range of approaches) → Cheng's
*Designing Type* (construction) → Lupton (anatomy and spacing), each with a drill from `practice-drills.md`
(e.g. the 30-letter drill or the spacing drill).

## 3. For history, range and taste

- **Jens Müller — *Logo Modernism* (Taschen, 2015).** Thousands of modernist trademarks (1940–1980) organised by
  form. Assign for range in geometric and abstract marks, and for the discipline of reduction.
- **Per Mollerup — *Marks of Excellence: The History and Taxonomy of Trademarks* (Phaidon; 1997, revised 2013).**
  A taxonomy of trademark types with history. Assign when the designer needs vocabulary for mark types.
- **Josef Müller-Brockmann — *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (1981).** For construction discipline and layout of
  guidelines/presentations.
- **Robert Bringhurst — *The Elements of Typographic Style*.** For the designer who's hooked on typography and
  wants depth.

## 4. Quick map: weakness → reading

| Seen in the work | Assign | With |
|---|---|---|
| Thin brief, weak strategy | Wheeler; Johnson | Rewrite the brief from a case study |
| First idea every time, narrow range | Evamy *Logo*; Müller *Logo Modernism* | 30-marks drill |
| Over-decorated, can't edit | *Identify*; Rand | Reduction drill |
| Wordmarks look like typed names | Evamy *Logotype*; Cheng | One-letter-change drill |
| Spacing and pairing problems | Lupton | Type-pairing drill |
| Colour does the heavy lifting | Albers | One-colour challenge |
| Perfectionism, low output | Draplin | Daily thumbnail drill |
| Nervous presenting, weak rationale | Airey; Rand's presentation booklets | Rationale rewrite (Stage 6) |
